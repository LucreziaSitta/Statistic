Step 3
Expected frequencies and the chi-square statistic

If X and Y were independent, every joint frequency would be the product of its marginals, fᵢⱼ = fᵢ₊·f₊ⱼ. Multiplying by N gives the counts expected under independence, with the same margins as the data:

Eᵢⱼ = N · fᵢ₊ · f₊ⱼ = nᵢ₊ · n₊ⱼ / N
Expected counts Eᵢⱼ
Contributions (nᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ)² / Eᵢⱼ  ▲ observed above expected, ▼ below

Pearson's statistic adds up the squared gaps, each divided by the expected count:

χ² = Σᵢ Σⱼ (nᵢⱼ − Eᵢⱼ)² / Eᵢⱼ,   df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

χ² = 0 exactly when the table equals the independence table, and grows with the distance from it. If X and Y are independent in the population and the expected counts are not too small (all ≥ 5 is the usual rule), χ² follows approximately a chi-square distribution with df degrees of freedom. The p-value is the probability, under independence, of a value at least as large as the observed one.

Step 4
Independence: theory, interpretation, causality

In probability

Two events are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B), that is, when P(B) > 0, P(A | B) = P(A): knowing that B happened does not change the probability of A. Two discrete random variables are independent when P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x)·P(Y = y) for every pair of values. This is an exact property of a model, and it is symmetric in X and Y.

In statistics

On data the same definition reads fᵢⱼ = fᵢ₊·f₊ⱼ, or equivalently: all the row profiles f(Y | X) are equal to the marginal of Y. In a sample this almost never holds exactly, because of random variation. So the question becomes a test: H₀ X and Y are independent in the population, H₁ they are not. χ² measures whether the gap from the independence table is larger than chance would produce.

Interpretation of the current dataset
  • A small p-value says the association is unlikely to be due to chance; it does not say how strong it is. Cramér's V = √(χ² / (N·(min(r, c) − 1))), between 0 and 1, measures the strength, while χ² also grows with N.
  • A large p-value does not prove independence: it only means that the data are compatible with it. With the random sample under independence, about α of the seeds (5% with α = 0.05) still reject H₀: try several seeds.
  • The p-value is not the probability that H₀ is true.
Association is not causality

The chi-square test detects statistical association: the distribution of Y changes with X. It is symmetric in X and Y, so it cannot even say which one would be the cause, and it cannot say why they are associated. In the login example, more high alerts from public Wi-Fi does not prove that the network causes the alerts:

  • Confounding: sessions from public Wi-Fi may come mostly from staff who travel and use personal or older devices; the device, not the network, could produce the alerts.
  • Measurement: the monitoring system may itself give a higher base score to unknown networks, so part of the association is built into how Y is recorded.
  • Selection: only sessions that reached the gateway are in the data; blocked attempts are missing.

A causal conclusion would need a design that removes these alternatives, for example comparing the same devices and users across networks, or a controlled experiment. What the test establishes is narrower but still useful: whether the association is larger than sampling noise, and in which cells it is located (the largest contributions in step 3).
