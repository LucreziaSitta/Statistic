Step 3
Expected frequencies and the chi-square test

Independence says that the joint frequency of a cell is the product of the two marginal ones, fᵢⱼ = fᵢ₊·f₊ⱼ. Multiplying by N turns this into the counts that the table should contain if X and Y were independent, with exactly the same totals as the data:

Eᵢⱼ = N · fᵢ₊ · f₊ⱼ = nᵢ₊ · n₊ⱼ / N

  • Degrees of freedom. The expected table keeps the row and column totals of the data. Once those are fixed, only (r − 1)(c − 1) cells can be filled freely and the others are forced by the totals: in a 2 × 3 table, choosing 2 cells of the first row decides everything else.   • p-value. If X and Y are independent and the expected counts are not too small (the usual rule: at least 5 in every cell, or at most 20% of the cells below 5 and none below 1), χ² follows approximately the chi-square distribution with df degrees of freedom. The p-value is the probability, under independence, of a χ² at least as large as the observed one: Q(df/2, χ²/2), the regularized upper incomplete gamma function, computed with its series and continued fraction.
  • Strength. χ² measures how strong the evidence of dependence is, and it grows with N. To measure how strong the dependence itself is, I use Cramér's V = √(χ² / (N·(min(r, c) − 1))), from 0 (independent in the data) to 1.

How many records does the test need?

The same question can get different answers depending only on how much data we have. To see it, I treat two tables as whole populations and draw from each one many random samples of size n, applying the χ² test to every sample:

  • a population with an association: the proportions of the table of step 2;
  • a population without association: the same margins, but every row with the same profile (the expected table of step 3).

The share of samples where H₀ is rejected is plotted against n. For the first population it is the probability of detecting a dependence that exists (power); for the second it is the probability of a false alarm (type I error), which should stay close to α.

Step 6
Independence: theory, interpretation, causality

In probability

Two events A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B), and, if P(B) > 0, this is the same as P(A | B) = P(A): being told that B happened does not change the probability of A.

An example with one die. A = "even", B = "at most 4": P(A ∩ B) = P({2, 4}) = 2/6 and P(A)·P(B) = ½ · 4/6 = 2/6, so they are independent, even though they concern the same throw. With C = "at most 3" instead: P(A ∩ C) = P({2}) = 1/6, but P(A)·P(C) = 1/4, so A and C are dependent. Independence is a property of the probabilities, not of whether the events "look related".

Two random variables are independent when P(X = xᵢ, Y = yⱼ) = P(X = xᵢ)·P(Y = yⱼ) for every pair of values, so every conditional distribution of Y equals its marginal distribution.

In statistics

On a dataset the same idea reads fᵢⱼ = fᵢ₊·f₊ⱼ in every cell, which means nᵢⱼ = Eᵢⱼ, χ² = 0 and all the gaps of step 2 equal to 0. Real data almost never satisfy this exactly: counts are integers and every sample is a little different.

So the statistical question is about the population the records come from: H₀: X and Y are independent, H₁: they are not. The test asks whether the distance between the data and the independence table is larger than sampling alone would produce. A small p-value is evidence of dependence; a large p-value is only absence of evidence, as step 4 shows with small samples.

The test also assumes that the records are independent of one another (each patient or session counted once): a different requirement from the independence of X and Y that is being tested.

Interpretation of the current table
Statistical association and causality

The χ² test says whether two variables are associated. It is symmetric in X and Y and uses only counts, so on its own it says nothing about why. My two datasets show the difference between the situations in which a causal reading is possible and those in which it is not.

Login sessions: observational data

Nobody decided where each session came from. Users who connect from public Wi-Fi may differ in many ways from those in the office: they travel, use personal laptops, work at odd hours. Any of these could explain more high alerts, and so could the monitoring rules themselves, if they score unknown networks higher. The association is real in the data, but the table cannot tell which of these explanations is the right one.

Arthritis trial: a randomised experiment

Here the treatment was assigned at random and double blind. Randomisation makes the treatment independent of everything about the patient, measured or not, and the data confirm it for sex (step 5, p = ). The only systematic difference between the two groups is then the drug, so the association between Treatment and Improved can be read as an effect of the treatment: the reason clinical trials are randomised.

In between, with observational data, a causal claim needs a model of the possible confounders and data to adjust for them, for example by comparing groups that are alike in those variables (as done for sex in step 5). And the absence of association does not exclude an effect: in a small sample it can simply be missed.
