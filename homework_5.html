Ex. 1 · Welford's recurrence formulas
Updating the mean, SS and the variance one score at a time
KnuthWelfordone pass

Scores x₁, x₂, … arrive one at a time. After k of them we want the mean, the sum of squared deviations and the sample variance

Meanx̄ₖ = (1/k) Σ xᵢsum over i = 1 … k
Sum of squaresSSₖ = Σ (xᵢ − x̄ₖ)²deviations from the current mean
Sample variances²ₖ = SSₖ / (k − 1)defined for k ≥ 2

without storing the scores and without going through them twice. All the formulas below use the same quantity, the gap between the new score and the old mean: dₖ = xₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁.

① The mean: Knuth's recurrence

The sum of the first k scores is the sum of the first k − 1 plus the new one: k·x̄ₖ = (k − 1)·x̄ₖ₋₁ + xₖ. Writing (k − 1)·x̄ₖ₋₁ = k·x̄ₖ₋₁ − x̄ₖ₋₁ and dividing by k:

x̄ₖ = x̄ₖ₋₁ + dₖ / k,   x̄₀ = 0

Two facts that come straight from it and are needed below: the mean moves by x̄ₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁ = dₖ/k, and the new score is at distance xₖ − x̄ₖ = dₖ − dₖ/k = ((k − 1)/k)·dₖ from the new mean.

② The sum of squares: same idea, "old value + correction"

Split SSₖ into the old scores and the new one: SSₖ = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ⁻¹ (xᵢ − x̄ₖ)² + (xₖ − x̄ₖ)². For the old scores, measure from the old mean and correct for the shift of the mean, xᵢ − x̄ₖ = (xᵢ − x̄ₖ₋₁) − (x̄ₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁):

Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ⁻¹ (xᵢ − x̄ₖ)² = SSₖ₋₁ − 2(x̄ₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁)·Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ⁻¹ (xᵢ − x̄ₖ₋₁) + (k − 1)(x̄ₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁)²

The middle sum is zero, because deviations from their own mean add up to zero. Using x̄ₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁ = dₖ/k and xₖ − x̄ₖ = ((k − 1)/k)·dₖ:

SSₖ = SSₖ₋₁ + (k − 1)·dₖ²/k² + (k − 1)²·dₖ²/k² = SSₖ₋₁ + dₖ²·(k − 1)(1 + k − 1)/k² = SSₖ₋₁ + ((k − 1)/k)·dₖ²

and since ((k − 1)/k)·dₖ is exactly xₖ − x̄ₖ, the correction is the product of the gap from the old mean and the gap from the new mean:

SSₖ = SSₖ₋₁ + dₖ · (xₖ − x̄ₖ) = SSₖ₋₁ + (xₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁)(xₖ − x̄ₖ),   SS₀ = SS₁ = 0

The two gaps always have the same sign, so the correction is never negative: SS can only grow, as a sum of squares must.

③ The variance

Substituting SSₖ = (k − 1)s²ₖ and SSₖ₋₁ = (k − 2)s²ₖ₋₁ in ② and dividing by k − 1:

s²ₖ = ((k − 2)/(k − 1))·s²ₖ₋₁ + dₖ²/k,   k ≥ 2 (so s²₂ = d₂²/2)

For the "divide by k" variance σ̂²ₖ = SSₖ/k the recurrence takes exactly Knuth's shape, old value plus (new term − old value)/k:

σ̂²ₖ = σ̂²ₖ₋₁ + ( dₖ·(xₖ − x̄ₖ) − σ̂²ₖ₋₁ ) / k
The code: three core lines
function welford(scores) { let k = 0, mean = 0, ss = 0; for (const x of scores) { k += 1; const d = x - mean; // d_k = x_k − mean_{k−1} (gap from the OLD mean) mean += d / k; // Knuth: mean_k = mean_{k−1} + d_k / k ss += d * (x - mean); // Welford: SS_k = SS_{k−1} + d_k · (x_k − mean_k) } return { n: k, mean, ss, variance: k > 1 ? ss / (k - 1) : NaN }; }

Order matters: d is computed with the old mean, the mean is updated, and only then SS uses the new mean. The same three lines run inside the simulation of Ex. 2, once per score of every hacker; step 2 checks the function against the textbook definition.

The same three lines in Python
mean = ss = 0.0 for k, x in enumerate(scores, start=1): d = x - mean mean += d / k ss += d * (x - mean) variance = ss / (k - 1)

Ex. 1 · Why not the textbook shortcut
Numerical stability
cancellationstable

The one-pass "shortcut" s² = (Σx² − (Σx)²/n) / (n − 1) is algebraically the same thing, and it also needs only one pass. But when the scores have a large common offset it subtracts two huge, almost equal numbers: double-precision numbers carry about 16 significant digits, and the difference keeps only the digits that survive. Welford only ever handles the small gaps dₖ, so the offset does not matter.

Below, the same seven scores 6, 9, 2, 7, 4, 10, 5 (exact s² = 164/21 = 7.8095…) are shifted by a growing constant c. Shifting does not change the variance, so every row should give 7.8095.

offset cWelford s²shortcut s²two-pass s²shortcut: relative error

The two-pass method (first the mean, then Σ(x − x̄)²) is also accurate, but it needs all the data twice. Welford is the only one of the three that is both one-pass and stable, which is what a program needs when scores arrive as a stream, as in the hacker simulation.

Ex. 2 · The experiment
m hackers, n systems, one score from 0 to 10 per attempt
populationsampleestimate

This extends the previous homework: instead of a 0/1 or ±1 increment, every penetration attempt now receives a score between 0 and 10, drawn from a fixed distribution chosen in the side panel. Its population mean μ and variance σ² are computed exactly from the probabilities. Each of the m hackers attacks n systems and collects a trajectory of n scores; Welford's recurrences, run along that trajectory, give the hacker's sampling mean x̄ and sampling variance s². Then the m values are averaged and compared with μ and σ².

1 · Generate

Each hacker draws n independent scores from the chosen distribution (one random stream, fixed seed).

2 · Welford per hacker

Three lines per score update x̄ and SS; at n = 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, … the current x̄ and s² = SS/(n − 1) are recorded.

3 · Compare across hackers

Why n − 1 and not n

The deviations are measured from x̄, which is computed from the same scores and therefore sits closer to them than the unknown μ does. On average E[SS] = (n − 1)·σ²: one "degree of freedom" is spent estimating μ. Dividing by n − 1 repays it exactly, so E[s²] = σ² for every n. Dividing by n gives E[SS/n] = ((n − 1)/n)·σ², too small by a factor that matters for small n (half the variance at n = 2) and disappears for large n: the orange curve.

Conclusions

1 · The averages hit the targets

For every n, even n = 2, the average of the m sampling means is within Monte Carlo error of μ, and the average of the sampling variances (÷ n − 1) is within error of σ². x̄ and s² are unbiased: E[x̄] = μ, E[s²] = σ². The ÷ n version is biased low by the factor (n − 1)/n. The remaining gaps are noise of size σ/√(nm) and sd(s²)/√m, and shrink when m grows.

2 · Why it matters for inference

In practice μ and σ² are unknown and there is one sample: one x̄ and one s². Unbiasedness says these numbers are right on average over all the samples we could have drawn, so they are legitimate estimates. Since Var(x̄) = σ²/n, the standard error σ/√n can itself be estimated by s/√n: this is what confidence intervals (x̄ ± t·s/√n, about 95% coverage in the table) and tests are built on. Welford provides x̄ and s² from a stream, in one pass and without losing precision.

3 · Effect of n

Increasing the number of systems per hacker makes every single estimate more precise: the spread of x̄ is σ/√n, the spread of s² about √(μ₄ − σ⁴)/√n. Four times more systems halve both. The estimators are consistent: the histograms close in on μ and σ² and become bell-shaped (CLT, as in HMWK 4). The variance is harder to estimate than the mean: its spread depends on the fourth moment, so skewed or all-or-nothing score distributions make s² noisier.

4 · Effect of m

More hackers do not make any individual estimate better; they make our picture of the estimators sharper: the averages in the table get closer to μ and σ², and the measured spreads closer to the theoretical ones. In a real study the analogue of m is the number of independent repetitions of the whole experiment.

📊 Theory notes
1. Population vs sample
The population is the score distribution itself, with parameters μ = Σ x·p(x), σ² = Σ (x − μ)² p(x), μ₄ = Σ (x − μ)⁴ p(x). A sample is the n scores of one hacker; x̄ and s² are statistics: numbers computed from the sample, random because the sample is random. An estimator is a statistic used to guess a parameter.
2. E[x̄] = μ and Var(x̄) = σ²/n
By linearity E[x̄] = (1/n) Σ E[Xᵢ] = μ. For independent scores the variances add: Var(Σ Xᵢ) = nσ², so Var(x̄) = nσ²/n² = σ²/n. The standard deviation σ/√n is the standard error of the mean.
3. E[SS] = (n − 1)σ²
Write Xᵢ − x̄ = (Xᵢ − μ) − (x̄ − μ). Then SS = Σ (Xᵢ − μ)² − n(x̄ − μ)² (the cross term gives −2n(x̄ − μ)²). Taking expectations: E[SS] = nσ² − n·(σ²/n) = (n − 1)σ². Hence E[SS/(n − 1)] = σ² (unbiased) and E[SS/n] = ((n − 1)/n)σ² (biased low). This is Bessel's correction.
4. Variance of s²
Var(s²) = (1/n)·(μ₄ − ((n − 3)/(n − 1))·σ⁴), exact for every distribution with a finite fourth moment. For large n it is ≈ (μ₄ − σ⁴)/n. For normal data μ₄ = 3σ⁴ and it becomes 2σ⁴/(n − 1). With kurtosis κ = μ₄/σ⁴, the relative spread of s² is about √((κ − 1)/n): flat or two-peaked distributions (small κ) give more stable variance estimates than heavy-tailed ones.
5. Unbiased, consistent, standard error
  • Unbiased: the estimator's expected value equals the parameter, for every n.
  • Consistent: the estimator converges in probability to the parameter as n → ∞. By Chebyshev, P(|x̄ − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²) → 0; the same works for s² because Var(s²) → 0.
  • Standard error: the standard deviation of an estimator. Unknown σ is replaced by s, giving s/√n, and with it intervals x̄ ± t·s/√n, where t is Student's quantile with n − 1 degrees of freedom (1.96 for large n).
6. Combining two hackers' statistics
Welford's idea extends to merging two partial results (Chan, Golub and LeVeque): with counts nₐ, n_b, means x̄ₐ, x̄_b and δ = x̄_b − x̄ₐ,
  • n = nₐ + n_b,   x̄ = x̄ₐ + δ·n_b/n
  • SS = SSₐ + SS_b + δ²·nₐn_b/n
With n_b = 1 this is exactly ② of step 1. It allows computing the statistics of all m·n scores in parallel, hacker by hacker.
