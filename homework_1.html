5. Theory & ECC context
What is elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC): public-key cryptography based on the algebraic structure of elliptic curves over finite fields (ℤp).

Scalar multiplication (Q = k·G): adding the base point G to itself k times using the chord (addition) and tangent (doubling) rules.

ECDLP: computing Q from k and G is easy (O(log k) with double-and-add); recovering k from Q and G is infeasible on large curves, which secures Ethereum and Bitcoin.
5b. Point addition, doubling & infinity
Summary. Curve y² = x³ + 7 (mod 17), G = (1,5), k = 6 → Q = 6G = (5,8). The curve has 17 points plus infinity (18 elements); G has order 9, so the sequence repeats every 9 steps. The statistics come from this periodicity and the tiny group, not from real randomness.

Addition P₁≠P₂: λ = (y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁), x₃ = λ²−x₁−x₂, y₃ = λ(x₁−x₃)−y₁ (mod 17).
Doubling P₁=P₂: λ = (3x₁²+a)/(2y₁); here a = 0. "Division" means multiplying by the modular inverse (e.g. 10⁻¹ ≡ 12 since 120 = 7·17+1).

2G: λ = 3·1²/10 ≡ 3·12 ≡ 2; x₃ = 4−1−1 = 2; y₃ = 2(1−2)−5 = −7 ≡ 10 → (2,10).
3G = 2G+G: λ = (10−5)/(2−1) = 5; x₃ = 25−1−2 ≡ 5; y₃ = 5(1−5)−5 = −25 ≡ 9 → (5,9).

Point at infinity (∞): neutral element, P + ∞ = P. It appears when adding P and −P = (x,−y), e.g. G + 8G = (1,5)+(1,12) = ∞, so order(G) = 9.

Double-and-add: k = 6 = 110₂. Bit 0 = 0 (skip), bit 1 = 1 (add 2G), bit 2 = 1 (add 4G): 2G+4G = (2,10)+(12,1) = 6G = (5,8) — same as 6 sequential additions. For a random 256-bit k it needs ≈256 doublings and on average ≈128 additions (one per 1-bit of k, at most 256) instead of ~2256 sequential additions.

Why there is no curve on the 𝔽17 grid: over a finite field the "curve" is just the finite set of 17 solutions (plus ∞). There is no continuity, so no line or curve can be drawn through them, and λ is a modular fraction, not a geometric slope. The right-hand panel draws y² = x³ + 7 over the real numbers (the shape of secp256k1) only as an analogy: the chord-and-tangent rule is the same formula, but the real points do not correspond to the 𝔽17 points. It also shows why cryptography uses a finite field: real multiples of P quickly get enormous coordinates (11P has x ≈ 3824) and lose floating-point precision, while 𝔽p coordinates always stay in 0…p−1 and are computed exactly.

Private keys live modulo n: since nG = ∞, (k + n)G = kG. With n = 9, k = 15 and k = 6 give the same Q = (5,8), and k = 9 gives Q = ∞. That is why brute force on k = 15 returns 6 (15 ≡ 6 mod 9), and why real wallets require 1 ≤ k ≤ n−1.

Why only a toy: the curve group has 18 elements and G generates a subgroup of order n = 9, so trying k = 1,2,3,… recovers k in at most 9 steps. On secp256k1 the group order is ≈2256 and the best generic attack (Pollard's rho) needs ≈2128 operations.

8. Discussion: one-way function vs hash
Meaningful for a one-way function (ECDLP): (a) hardness of inversion — the only real security property; the brute-force box shows it costs ≤ n steps here but ≈ 2128 on secp256k1. (b) Uniformity of Q over the subgroup, so Q leaks no bias about k. (c) Absence of simple correlation between k and coordinates (r≈0). These are necessary but not sufficient: statistics can't prove hardness.

Hash-specific properties: avalanche (flipping 1 input bit flips ~50% of output bits), arbitrary-length input compressed to fixed output, collision and second-preimage resistance. kG is not designed for these: it is injective on 1…n−1 (no collisions at all), and Qk+1=Qk+G, so neighbouring keys give structured, not avalanche-like, outputs. The measured avalanche for kG (≈0.49) is almost the same as for the hash stand-in (≈0.46) simply because any two of only 8 distinct points, encoded in 10 bits, already differ in about half their bits; it is not a security argument.

Algebraic structure is a feature here, a flaw for hashes: (a+b)G = aG + bG enables ECDH and signatures, whereas a hash must have no such homomorphism. Also x(kG) = x((n−k)G) (Q and −Q share x), so x alone is a 2-to-1 map — visible in the x-histogram when k covers a full cycle 1…n−1 (set k = 8 for the default G: each x appears exactly twice). This is also why compressed public keys store only x plus one parity bit for y.

Caveat: the toy curve is tiny, so χ² and correlations are small-sample illustrations only. In Ethereum the address is then keccak-256(Q) — the hash is applied after the ECC step.

What matters for the public key: determinism, efficient forward computation, a huge group, and hardness of inversion. 8c. Why these statistical tools (and their limits)
The key fact: k ↦ kG is a bijection. On k = 1…n−1 every point of the subgroup ⟨G⟩ (except ∞) is hit exactly once. So uniformity of Q over the points is a theorem, not a statistical finding: if k is chosen uniformly at random, Q is uniform. This is exactly the property that matters cryptographically. Running a test on the deterministic sweep k = 1,2,…,n−1 is degenerate (try G = (15,13), order 18: χ² for y is exactly 0). A statistical test only makes sense on randomly sampled keys, and even then it mainly checks the random number generator.

χ² goodness-of-fit. It compares observed counts Oi with expected counts Ei under a null model: χ² = Σ (Oi − Ei)² / Ei, approximately χ²-distributed with (cells − 1) degrees of freedom. Two conditions must hold: (1) the null model must be the right one — ⟨G⟩ for G = (1,5) only reaches x ∈ {1,2,5,12}, so testing against all 9 x-values of the curve is wrong; (2) every expected count should be at least about 5, otherwise the χ² approximation breaks down (the original sweep had expected counts 0.89 and 0.47). Section 7 therefore reports the naive test as invalid and runs a proper test on N random keys. The p-value is the probability of a χ² at least this large if the null model were true; p > 0.05 means "no evidence against uniformity", not "proof of uniformity".

Why x is not uniform but y looks uniform. Q and −Q = (x, −y) share x, so each x appears twice (once only when y = 0), while y-values are all distinct within the subgroup. Entropy reflects this: H(X) = 2.00 bits vs H(Y) = 3.00 bits for k = 1…8. Under random sampling, the correct null for x gives each x a weight proportional to how many subgroup points have that x.

Entropy. H = −Σ pi log₂ pi measures the uncertainty of the observed distribution (maximum log₂ of the number of outcomes). It says nothing about computational hardness: the trivial bijection k ↦ k + 1 also has maximal entropy.

Pearson correlation. r only detects linear dependence. Here Q is a deterministic function of k, yet r ≈ 0, because the relation is modular (wrap-around), not linear. Low r is necessary, not sufficient, for "no leakage".

Avalanche (Strict Avalanche Criterion). A hash or block cipher is designed so that flipping one input bit flips each output bit with probability ½. kG is not designed for this: Qk+1 = Qk + G is pure structure. On the toy curve, values near 0.5 appear for both kG and the hash stand-in because there are so few distinct outputs; the comparison shows the metric has no discriminating power here.

Bit balance and encoding bias. Coordinates lie in 0…p−1 with p = 17, encoded on 5 bits. Only the value 16 has bit 4 set, so even perfectly uniform values give P(bit4 = 1) = 1/17 ≈ 0.06. This bias comes from the encoding (p is not a power of 2), not from scalar multiplication. For secp256k1, p = 2256 − 232 − 977 is so close to 2256 that the bias is about 2−224, i.e. negligible.

Comparing base points. The order of G decides everything: with order 18 (e.g. G = (15,13)) the subgroup is the whole curve and all 17 affine points appear; with order 3 or 2 the "public key" can take only 2 or 1 values. Real curves use a base point of huge prime order for exactly this reason.
9. Conclusions
1. Coordinate distribution. kG maps each private key deterministically to a point in the subgroup generated by G. Individual coordinates are not necessarily uniform: x takes only a few distinct values (Q and −Q share x), while y is more varied.

2. Bits and entropy. Bit frequencies are unbalanced mainly because of the encoding: values 0…16 on 5 bits make the top bit almost always 0, whatever the algorithm (negligible on secp256k1). Entropy measures uncertainty of the observed distribution (e.g. 2.00 bits for x vs 3.00 bits for y at k=1…8); it is not computational hardness.

3. Correlation and avalanche. Low Pearson correlation shows no linear relation but not independence. Flipping one bit of k may change many output bits, yet the classical avalanche criterion belongs to hashes; here Qk+1 = Qk + G is structured.

4. One-way vs hash. kG is efficient forward (double-and-add) and hard to invert on large curves (ECDLP). A hash maps arbitrary input to fixed output with pre-image, second-pre-image and collision resistance; kG is not a general-purpose hash and keeps algebraic structure.

5. Uniformity and the χ² test. Uniformity of Q over ⟨G⟩ follows from the bijection k ↦ kG; the χ² test is only meaningful on randomly sampled keys, against the correct null model and with expected counts ≥ 5.

6. Security limitations. This toy subgroup has very few points, so exhaustive search recovers k instantly. Real Ethereum uses secp256k1 (≈2256 points) with a carefully chosen base point.
