Step 1 · The model
n systems, m attackers, one random score per step
IID incrementsBernoulliRademacher

Every attacker goes through the same n systems, one after the other. At system i the attacker gets a random penetration score Xᵢ; the scores X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ are independent and all have the same distribution. After k systems the attacker has accumulated Sₖ = X₁ + … + Xₖ. Simulating m attackers gives m independent trajectories of this process, and the spread of those m values is our picture of the distribution of Sₖ.

Bernoulli model

Xᵢ ∈ {0, 1}, P(Xᵢ = 1) = p

1: system i is penetrated; 0: the attempt fails. Sₙ counts the penetrated systems and Sₙ/n is the fraction of the n systems that fell.

μ = p,   σ² = p(1 − p). With the current p:

Rademacher model

Xᵢ ∈ {−1, +1}, each with probability ½

+1: the attacker gains ground; −1: the defence pushes it back by the same amount. Sₙ is the net progress after n systems.

μ = 0,   σ² = 1: the symmetric, zero-mean case, where the Central Limit Theorem needs no centering or rescaling.

The three quantities we study
Total scoreSₙ = Σ Xᵢmean nμ · variance nσ²
Average scoreSₙ / nmean μ · variance σ²/n
Normalized total scoreSₙ / √nmean μ√n · variance σ²

All three are the same random sum seen on different scales. The variances follow from two facts only: for independent increments Var(Sₙ) = nσ², and Var(aY) = a²·Var(Y). The next steps check these formulas on the simulated attackers.

How to read n and m

n: number of systems

The length of one attack. Increasing n is what lets the limit theorems appear: they are statements about n → ∞.

m: number of attackers

The number of independent trajectories. A larger m does not change the law of one attacker; it gives a sharper empirical picture of it (step 7).

Suitable choices of m

To see a distribution, m must be large compared with the number of histogram bins: a few hundred is the minimum, a few thousand gives smooth histograms.

Step 4 · Law of Large Numbers
Sₙ/n converges in probability to μ
convergence in probabilityChebyshev

The weak Law of Large Numbers says that for every tolerance ε > 0 P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) → 0 as n → ∞. For the Bernoulli model the fraction of penetrated systems approaches p; for the Rademacher model the average progress approaches 0. We fix a tolerance ε and count, at every k, how many of the m attackers are still outside the corridor μ ± ε.

ε = 0.04

Why Chebyshev works. Since E[Sₙ/n] = μ and Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n, Chebyshev's inequality gives P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²), which goes to 0. This is the whole proof of the weak LLN for increments with finite variance. The bound is valid for every n but loose; the normal approximation, which comes from the CLT, is much closer to the simulation.

What the LLN does not say. The average settles, but the total score does not: in the Bernoulli model Sₙ − np keeps fluctuating by about √(np(1−p)), and in the Rademacher model Sₙ wanders further and further from 0 (step 2). The total is predictable only relative to n.

Step 5 · Variance under the three normalizations
nσ², σ²/n and σ²
slope +1slope −1slope 0

For independent increments the variances add up, so Var(Sₖ) = kσ². Dividing a random variable by a constant c divides its variance by c²: dividing by k gives σ²/k, dividing by √k gives exactly σ². On a log–log plot the three laws are straight lines with slopes +1, −1 and 0. The dots are the variances measured on the m simulated attackers at each k.

Reading the three scales. Dividing by n is too strong: it kills the fluctuations, and only the mean survives (LLN). Not dividing at all is too weak: the fluctuations grow without bound. Dividing by √n is exactly in between: fluctuations keep a fixed size, which is why √n is the scale at which their shape can be studied (CLT).

Step 6 · Central Limit Theorem
Sₙ/√n → N(0, 1) in the Rademacher case
convergence in distributionQ–Q plotKolmogorov–Smirnov

For Rademacher increments μ = 0 and σ = 1, so the CLT (Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) → N(0, 1) becomes simply Sₙ/√n → N(0, 1). Convergence in distribution is about the shape of the histogram, not about single values: every attacker keeps a random score, but the proportion of attackers in any interval approaches the normal probability of that interval. This step always uses the Rademacher simulation; the switch shows the standardized Bernoulli score for comparison.

Why the discreteness disappears. Sₖ/√k can only take values on a grid with spacing 2/√k. For small k the histogram is a few separated bars and the Q–Q plot a staircase; as k grows the grid becomes finer than the histogram bins and the bars merge into the bell curve. The individual increments never become normal: only the distribution of the normalized sum does.

n and m play different roles. n changes the random variable itself (a longer attack), so it is what drives the limit theorems. m changes how many independent copies of it we observe, so it controls how precisely the simulation estimates the theoretical quantities, and how extreme the best and worst attackers are.

Aggregate penetration score of all the attackers

Adding up the totals of all attackers gives T = S⁽¹⁾ₙ + … + S⁽ᵐ⁾ₙ, a sum of n·m independent increments: its mean is nmμ and its variance nmσ². The score per attacker and per system, T/(nm), is therefore an average of n·m increments and is pinned to μ with standard deviation σ/√(nm).

Best and worst attackers

Even when every attacker has the same law, the most successful one among m is far above the mean. For approximately normal scores, the largest of m standardized values is about Φ⁻¹((m − 0.375)/(m + 0.25)) (Blom's approximation) and grows like √(2 ln m): slowly, but without limit. More attackers do not change the typical attacker, they make an exceptional one more likely.

Conclusions
  1. Single steps stay unpredictable. No amount of data predicts whether the next system falls; regularity appears only in aggregates.
  2. The average is predictable. Sₙ/n converges in probability to μ: p for Bernoulli, 0 for Rademacher. The simulated share of attackers outside μ ± ε falls to 0, faster than Chebyshev guarantees.
  3. The normalization decides what survives. Var Sₙ = nσ² grows, Var Sₙ/n = σ²/n vanishes, Var Sₙ/√n = σ² stays put; the log–log slopes measured on the simulation are +1, −1 and 0.
  4. The fluctuations become Gaussian. In the Rademacher case Sₙ/√n approaches N(0, 1): the Q–Q plot straightens, skewness and kurtosis approach 0 and the KS distance falls, until it is limited only by the number of attackers.
  5. Interpretation. A larger system makes the rate of penetration predictable but the absolute damage larger and more variable; a larger population of attackers leaves the typical attacker unchanged, sharpens what we can measure, and raises the score of the best one.

A Rademacher variable is 2B − 1 with B ~ Bernoulli(½), so the Rademacher walk is a shifted and doubled count of successes: Sₙ = 2·Binomial(n, ½) − n.
2. Variance of a sum
For independent X₁ … Xₙ the covariances vanish, so Var(Sₙ) = Σ Var(Xᵢ) = nσ². With Var(aY) = a² Var(Y):
  • Var(Sₙ/n) = nσ²/n² = σ²/n
  • Var(Sₙ/√n) = nσ²/n = σ²
Skewness of Sₙ decays like 1/√n and excess kurtosis like 1/n: the cumulants of order r scale as n, so after normalizing by √n the r-th standardized cumulant scales as n^(1 − r/2). Only the first two survive, which is the CLT in one line.
3. Law of Large Numbers
Weak LLN: for every ε > 0, P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) → 0 (convergence in probability).
Proof with finite variance: Chebyshev's inequality applied to Sₙ/n, whose mean is μ and variance σ²/n: P(|Sₙ/n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²).
Strong LLN: Sₙ/n → μ with probability 1, that is, almost every single trajectory of the running average eventually stays in any corridor around μ. The running-average plot of step 4 is a picture of this.
4. Two kinds of convergence
In probability (LLN): the random variable itself gets close to a constant. In distribution (CLT): only the probabilities P(Zₙ ≤ z) approach those of a limit law; the values stay random.

In the Bernoulli model Sₙ/√n does not converge in distribution: its variance is p(1 − p) but its mean p√n goes to infinity. The CLT applies to the centered and scaled version (Sₙ − np)/√(np(1 − p)). In the Rademacher model μ = 0 and σ = 1, so Sₙ/√n is already that version.
5. Central Limit Theorem and Berry–Esseen
Lindeberg–Lévy CLT: if the Xᵢ are IID with mean μ and finite variance σ² > 0, then (Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) → N(0, 1) in distribution.
Berry–Esseen: with ρ = E|X − μ|³, sup_z |P(Zₙ ≤ z) − Φ(z)| ≤ C·ρ/(σ³√n), with C ≤ 0.4748. For Rademacher this is 0.4748/√n: the distance to the normal law shrinks like 1/√n. For a lattice variable this order cannot be improved, because the distribution function of Zₙ has jumps of size about 1/√n.
6. Checking normality
  • Histogram vs density: bars scaled to density (area 1) next to φ(z). Bins are aligned with the lattice of possible values so that no bin gets an extra value by chance.
  • Q–Q plot: the i-th smallest value against Φ⁻¹((i − ½)/m). A straight 45° line means the same distribution; curvature shows skewness or heavy tails, steps show discreteness.
  • Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance D = sup |F̂ₘ(z) − Φ(z)|. With m exact normal values D is typically below 1.36/√m (5% critical value), so for large k the remaining D is mostly sampling error of the m attackers.
7. The best of m attackers
If Z₁ … Zₘ are independent N(0, 1), P(max ≤ z) = Φ(z)ᵐ. The maximum is near the quantile Φ⁻¹(1 − 1/m), approximately Φ⁻¹((m − 0.375)/(m + 0.25)) (Blom), and grows like √(2 ln m). Ten times more attackers move the best one up by only a fraction of a standard deviation, but always upward.
8. Timeline
  • 1713Jacob Bernoulli, Ars Conjectandi (posthumous): the first Law of Large Numbers, for Bernoulli trials.
  • 1733De Moivre: the normal curve as approximation of the binomial with p = ½, the first CLT.
  • 1812Laplace, Théorie analytique des probabilités: normal approximation for general p.
  • 1837Poisson names the "law of large numbers".
  • 1867Chebyshev: the inequality and an LLN for general variables (Bienaymé had a similar inequality in 1853).
  • 1901Lyapunov: rigorous CLT under moment conditions.
  • 1920Pólya calls it the "central limit theorem".
  • 1922Lindeberg's condition; Rademacher introduces the ±1 functions named after him.
  • 1933Kolmogorov: axioms of probability and the KS statistic.
  • 1941Berry (1941) and Esseen (1942): the rate of convergence in the CLT.
  • 1949Metropolis and Ulam publish "The Monte Carlo Method": simulating many random trajectories, as here.
